There is a version of “having taste” that sounds like classification: knowing which records belong in which subgenre, which books are canonical, which directors matter, which coffee table books are worth leaving out when people come over. I like classification. I spend enough of my working life trying to define things carefully that the impulse follows me home.

But the more music I collect and the more books I finish, the less I think taste is a taxonomy.

I think it is an archive.

Not an archive in the grand institutional sense. Mine is messier: music files with edited metadata, a Bandcamp collection, screenshots, book notes, a few underlined pages, lists of things friends recommended, concerts I almost skipped, and a Notion field called “How I found it.” The interesting part is not just what is in the archive. It is the trail that got each thing there.

That trail has become part of how I understand my artistic interests. The question is no longer only, *Do I like this?* It is: **What made me notice it, what made me keep it, and what makes me come back?**

### Notice

My music taste looks broad when I write it down. I listen to cinematic scores and soundtracks; acid, progressive, Italo, future, and other forms of house; pop and skate punk; hip hop; math rock; alternative rock. The labels are useful, but they make my listening sound more scattered than it feels.

What usually grabs me is more consistent than the genres: texture, movement, atmosphere, and a strong emotional identity. I like music that makes a place around itself.

That is probably why the most successful discovery methods for me are the ones where another person has left fingerprints.

Bandcamp made sense only after I stopped treating it like Spotify without an algorithm. The useful part is not a feed trying to infer my taste. It is the visible trail of other listeners: find an album, see who bought it, see what else those people bought, follow the collectors whose shelves start to look interesting. I have found music through Audiotree, House Fire Magazine, SoundCloud, and artists I saw opening for someone else. I went to see Japanese Breakfast in 2021 and nearly skipped the opener; that opener was Mannequin Pussy, who became one of my favorite bands.

The algorithmic tools still help. I use Discover Weekly. I like Spotify’s mixes. But they feel different. An algorithm gives me a candidate. A person gives me a story.

### Follow the trail

I realized I do the same thing with books because I have accidentally been recording provenance for years.

My book database has a “How I found it” field. At first that was just a practical note. Over time it became one of my favorite parts of the record.

Some books arrived through a direct recommendation. Some came out of a Reddit thread. Some appeared because another medium pointed backward toward them. I wanted to read *If Beale Street Could Talk* because I had heard the soundtrack first; music created a route into Baldwin. *The Old Man and the Sea* came out of looking for books about sailing, and then became the first book I can remember starting and finishing in a single day.

That last part matters because provenance is not the same thing as destiny. Where something came from gets it through the door. It does not guarantee that it stays.

I loved *The Old Man and the Sea*: the imagery, the adventure, even the sailing vocabulary. I liked *Abundance* much more than I expected, especially the research-heavy and economic parts. *Atomic Habits* was useful but familiar; I was more interested in the appendix than the main argument. *Why Women Have Better Sex Under Socialism* had historical examples I found genuinely interesting, but the ending felt more preachy than I wanted. I did not like *Notes from Underground* at all.

That is also curation.

A personal canon cannot just be a trophy case of things I approve of. If the archive is supposed to say anything about me, it has to include discernment: the things that survived contact with me, the things that changed shape after I spent time with them, and the things I am glad to have encountered without wanting to encounter again.

### Keep

Music has made the idea of “keeping” unusually literal for me.

I have used Spotify since 2013, but I still prefer owning files when I care about a song. Streaming is astonishingly convenient, but the relationship is conditional. Songs disappear. Licensing changes. Offline modes have rules. A saved track can still vanish from the service that was supposed to remember it for me.

Buying a file from Bandcamp, Qobuz, or 7digital feels different. I can fix the metadata, attach the album art I want, back it up, put it on my phone or my watch, and listen to it ten years from now without needing a platform to agree that it still exists.

The practical benefit is obvious. The more interesting benefit is psychological: **ownership creates friction in the right direction.**

When I buy something, organize it, back it up, and deliberately return to it, I am doing more than consuming it. I am making a small claim that the work deserves future attention.

Books make that claim physically. A file makes it digitally. A roll of film makes it chemically. The medium changes; the gesture is similar.

### Revisit

The real test of taste may be what happens on the second encounter.

First encounters are noisy. Timing matters too much. A recommendation comes with expectations. A book lands during a particular season of life. A track sounds perfect because it was playing in a particular room, on a particular drive, or at the end of a particular night.

Revisiting separates the work from some of that context while adding new context of its own.

A song I bought years ago is not the same song when I hear it after moving, finishing a project, ending a relationship, or discovering an entirely different genre. A book can become more or less convincing as I accumulate experiences the author could not know I would have.

This is one reason I increasingly like archives more than feeds. Feeds are optimized for the next thing. Archives make the previous thing available for another encounter.

And sometimes the previous thing gets better because *I* changed.

### Share

There is another stage that makes something feel permanently mine: explaining it to someone else.

My last music post was ostensibly about discovery tools, but writing it forced me to notice the philosophy underneath my habits. I thought I was describing websites and stores. I was really describing a preference for human curation, durable ownership, and visible provenance.

That is one reason I wanted to make this post broader.

The music, books, photographs, notes, concerts, and recommendations are different objects, but I am increasingly treating them as parts of the same practice. I notice something. I follow the trail. I keep what earns permanence. I revisit it. Eventually, I turn some of that curation into output: a blog post, a dinner conversation, a photograph, a recommendation to somebody else.

At that point, curation starts to look a lot like creation.

Not because selecting a record is the same as making one, or because annotating a book is the same as writing it. It is creative because arranging attention produces a point of view. The collection says something that no individual object says alone.

### The things I keep

I still like favorites lists. I still rank cities, albums, restaurants, gear, and almost anything else that can be made into a table. But I no longer think a list of favorites is the most interesting description of taste.

A better description is a durable record of attention.

What did I notice when there were a thousand other things I could have noticed? Who pointed me toward it? What did I think after spending real time with it? Did I want to own it? Did I return? Did I give it to someone else in the form of a recommendation?

The archive is never finished, which is probably the point.

I want to keep enough of a trail that, years from now, I can tell not only what I liked, but **how I became the person who liked it.**
