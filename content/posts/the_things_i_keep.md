I have been thinking about what it means to have taste. Not "good taste" in the status-signaling sense, but the much more personal question of why some things stick around while others disappear after a week.

I listen to a lot of music, I keep a running book list, I take film photos, and I like going to shows. The common thread is not really the medium. It is curation. I like finding things, remembering where they came from, deciding what deserves to stay, and then returning to them later with a slightly different version of myself.

That process has started to feel less like consumption and more like building a personal archive.

## Finding things is part of the thing

I wrote before about [how I find music](/posts/finding_new_music/), and the part I keep coming back to is that the route matters.

A recommendation algorithm can be useful. Spotify's Discover Weekly has absolutely found songs for me. But the discoveries I remember usually have fingerprints on them. I find an album on Bandcamp, click through the collections of people who liked it, and end up somewhere I would not have searched for directly. I sort Audiotree sessions by genre. I find a new DJ through a SoundCloud free download. I go to a concert for the headliner and leave thinking about the opener.

That last one is probably the clearest example. I went to see Japanese Breakfast in 2021 and almost skipped the opener. The opener was Mannequin Pussy, who became one of my favorite bands. The music is tied to the fact that I almost missed it.

Books have the same kind of provenance for me. *Wired for Music* came from a bookstore in Toronto. *My Year of Rest and Relaxation* was a recommendation from a friend. *The Old Man and the Sea* came out of looking for books about sailing. I read *If Beale Street Could Talk* because I found the soundtrack first and wanted to read the book and watch the movie before listening to it fully.

I keep a "How I found it" field in my book list, and I am realizing that field is almost as interesting to me as the title.

## Keeping music

My music taste is broad enough that genre labels stop being very useful after a point. I tend to circle around cinematic scores and soundtracks, house in a bunch of forms, pop and skate punk, hip hop, and math or alternative rock. The genres are different, but the songs I keep usually have a strong sense of texture, movement, atmosphere, or emotional identity.

The other thing I care about is permanence.

I have had a Spotify account since 2013, but I still like owning music files. Songs disappear from catalogs. Licensing changes. A release is available in one country and not another. "Offline" can still mean "available as long as the service says so."

Buying an album from Bandcamp, Qobuz, or 7digital and putting the files on my own drive changes the relationship a little. I can fix the metadata. I can back it up. I can put it on my watch and go for a run without a phone. I can find it years later because it is actually mine to keep.

There is something satisfying about pressing play and having the song start instantly because there is no service between me and the file.

## Keeping books

I am less systematic about books, but the notes I have kept are revealing.

I loved *The Old Man and the Sea*. It was the first time I can remember starting and finishing a book in one day. I liked the adventure, the imagery, and probably more sailing terminology than Hemingway expected me to care about.

I liked *Abundance* a lot because the science-and-research framing was stronger than I expected, and I was pleasantly surprised by how economically focused it was. I thought the conclusion drifted into a more preachy mode, but I still kept thinking about the core argument afterward.

*Why Women Have Better Sex Under Socialism* was similar for me: a strong start, historical examples I genuinely learned from, and an ending that moved away from what I personally wanted from the book.

*Atomic Habits* was useful without being revelatory. A lot of the concepts were familiar, but I can see why it works as an introduction. *Notes from Underground*, meanwhile, was a tough read for me. I finished it, but mostly came away thinking that I had spent a lot of time with a narrator I did not enjoy spending time with.

The point is not that every book needs to be "good." Some things earn a place in the archive because I loved them. Others stay because my reaction was strong enough to clarify what I do not like.

## Repetition is a form of editing

Music makes this obvious because repetition is built in. I can play the same song fifty times without thinking I am doing anything unusual. A song I liked casually can become attached to a summer, a run, a city, a person, or a period of work and then mean something different a year later.

Books make the same process slower. A line I underlined at 22 can sound completely different at 28. A book I found boring might suddenly become relevant because I finally have a question that the book is trying to answer.

Film photography works this way too. The useful part of a photograph is not always visible when I take it. Sometimes the roll matters because of what it accidentally preserved.

That is why "favorites" lists feel incomplete to me. A favorite is a snapshot. An archive has a history.

## An archive also needs deletion

Curation is partly deciding what not to keep.

There are albums I sampled because someone I trust liked them and never returned to. There are books I started twice and still did not enjoy. There are recommendation sites I expected to love but found too noisy. The failed paths matter because they make the successful ones more legible.

This is one reason I have grown to prefer hand-curated discovery over endlessly optimized feeds. A feed is designed to reduce friction. Personal curation needs a little friction. I want enough resistance to make a choice: follow this person, buy this record, finish this book, stop reading this one, save this photograph, write a note.

The act of choosing is the part that turns a pile of media into something personal.

## In closing

I do not think I am trying to build a definitive canon. I am trying to build a durable record of attention.

The system is pretty small:

1. Notice what catches my attention.
2. Keep track of how I found it.
3. Write down one reaction.
4. Own or save the things that matter when I can.
5. Revisit them on purpose.
6. Share the ones that still feel alive.

That last step is probably why I like writing these posts. The archive is more useful when it becomes a conversation.

So: what is something you keep returning to, and do you remember how you first found it?
